  The fixed α, β model in BNS (2025)

  What "fixed" means in the paper

  The model has a Cobb–Douglas production function with land (Z, fixed quantity), capital (K), and labour (L):

  Yₜ = Aₜ Zᵅ Kₜᵝ Lₜ^(1−α−β)

  "Fixed α, β" means the factor shares are constant over all time periods — they do not drift as the economy
  industrialises. This contrasts with the "changing α/β" baseline (nt=105) where shares are allowed to vary from ~1760
  onward.

  ---
  III.A — Identification of α and β

  The paper offers two routes depending on the specification number (nt):

  Route 1 — IV/reduced-form (nt < 200, e.g. nt=103 = fixed α/β IV)

  - The combined slope α̃ = α / (1−β) is not estimated freely — it is pinned by an IV estimate using movements in real
  wages and population 1340–1360 (Black Death shock):

  slope0 <- delta_iv   # passed to Stan as a data constant
  - In Stan: include_slope = 1 → include_apar = 0 (α is not a free parameter).
  - β is a free parameter with prior uniform(max(0, 1−1/slope0), 1).
  - α is recovered deterministically: α[1] = slope0 × (1 − β).
  - So the IV pinned slope disciplines both shares jointly; only β is sampled.

  Route 2 — Structural (nt ≥ 200, e.g. nt=203 = fixed α/β structural)

  - include_slope = 0 → include_apar = 1.
  - The simplex (α, β, 1−α−β) is sampled from a Dirichlet(1,1,1) prior (uniform over the simplex).
  - Both α and β are free parameters, disciplined jointly by the likelihood from wages, land rents, and capital data.

  ---
  How equations (8) and (9) identify fixed α

  The two-step structure

  Identification works in two stages: a pre-estimation IV step that pins the combined slope, then a Bayesian HMC step
  that separates α from β.

  ---
  Step 1 — Equation (9) justifies the instrument; the slope is computed before Stan

  Equation (9) is the Malthusian population law of motion:

  nt − nt−1 = ω + γ(wt−1 + dt−1) + ξt

  The plague shock ξ1t in equation (10)–(11) is explicitly modelled as exogenous (a mixture: with probability π, a
  fraction of the population dies from a Beta-distributed shock, otherwise zero). This is what makes the Black Death
  1340–1360 usable as an instrument: equation (9) asserts that the population drop was exogenous to productivity.

  In estimation.R before Stan is called:

  delta_iv <- -(w[D==1360] - w[D==1340]) / (l[D==1360] - l[D==1340])
  slope0 <- delta_iv    # Wald estimator, passed to Stan as a data constant

  This is a Wald estimator: exogenous Δn from the plague (equation 9) instruments for the endogenous labour supply in
  equation (8), identifying the slope α̃ = α/(1−β). slope0 is fixed data, not a sampled parameter.

  ---
  Step 2 — Equation (8) is the likelihood; HMC recovers α and β

  Equation (8) in logs (with capital demand substituted in) is:

  wt = φ + (1/(1−β)) ãt − (α/(1−β))(dt+nt) − (β/(1−β)) log(rt+δ)

  In Stan (malthus_model.stan line 223), the labor demand prediction lbdm is:

  lbdm = log(1-alphat-betat)
       + (betat.*log(betat) + atilde - (alphat).*(n+h) - betat.*log(r+delta)) ./ (1-betat);

  The wage likelihood (line 314) is:

  w ~ N(φ + lbdm, se2 ./ (1−betat))

  Since slope0 = α/(1−β) is a data constant, Stan imposes (line 150):

  alpha[1] = slope0 * (1 - beta0[1]);   // α = α̃(1−β)
  alpha[2] = beta0[1];                   // β is the free parameter

  So α is not sampled directly — only β is sampled, subject to:

  beta0 ~ uniform(max(0, 1-1/slope0), 1)   // line 243

  What separates α from β? The term (β/(1−β)) log(rt+δ) in equation (8). The interest rate rt varies over time and is
  observed. Given slope0 is fixed, variation in rt identifies β through the wage likelihood: a larger β means interest
  rate movements have a larger effect on wages. Once β is pinned, α = slope0 × (1−β).

  ---
  What the fixed α/β restriction adds

  For fixed α/β (nt=103, include_Z = 0):

  include_Z <- 0
  ii_alpha <- 1:T        # ALL time periods
  ii_drft  <- integer(0) # no drift periods

  In Stan (lines 174, 181):
  alphat[ii_alpha] = rep_vector(alpha[1], T);   // constant for all t
  betat[ii_alpha]  = rep_vector(alpha[2], T);   // constant for all t

  Crucially, the rent and capital demand likelihoods (lines 348–351) are only added when include_Z = 1. For fixed α/β,
  they are absent from the likelihood. So the only discipline on β is:
  - Its prior (uniform on admissible range)
  - The time-series variation of rt in the wage equation (8)

  The paper's main interest in this case is the slope α̃ = α/(1−β), not α and β individually. The fixed case serves as a
  robustness check against the changing α/β baseline (nt=105) where rent and capital data post-1760 also enter the
  likelihood and more sharply identify β.

  ---
  lbdm and the likelihood

  What lbdm is

  lbdm is the model's predicted log wage given current parameter values, minus the normalisation constant phi[1]. It is
  the right-hand side of equation (8) — the labour demand curve after capital demand has been substituted out —
  evaluated at the sampled parameters.

  No capital (include_K = 0, line 225):
  lbdm = log(1-alphat) + atilde - alphat.*(n+h)
  This matches equation (3): wt = φ̃ + ãt − α lt with lt = nt + dt = n + h.

  log(1-alphat) is the part of φ̃ = log(1-α) + α log Z that is not absorbed into phi[1] (log Z is normalised to 0).

  With capital (include_K = 1, line 223):
  lbdm = log(1-alphat-betat)
       + (betat.*log(betat) + atilde
          - alphat.*(n+h) - betat.*log(r+delta)) ./ (1-betat)
  This matches equation (5) — the version with capital demand substituted out:

  wt = φ̃' + (1/(1-β)) ãt  −  (α/(1-β))(nt+dt)  −  (β/(1-β)) log(rt+δ)

  where φ̃' = log(1-α-β) + β log β / (1-β) (log Z = 0).

  Expanding the code confirms:
  log(1-α-β)  +  [β log β + ãt − α(n+h) − β log(r+δ)] / (1-β)
  = φ̃'  +  (1/(1-β)) ãt  −  (α/(1-β))(n+h)  −  (β/(1-β)) log(r+δ)

  ---
  atilde — the permanent productivity state

  at = ãt + ε2t. The permanent component ãt is atilde in the code. For fixed α/β (include_Z = 0):

  atilde[ii_alpha] = mlq[ii_alpha]   // all t

  mlq (Malmquist log quantity index) is built from the sampled first-differences dmlq:

  mlq[1] = 0;
  mlq[t] = dmlq[t-1] + mlq[t-1];    // cumulative sum of dmlq

  dmlq[t] = ãt − ãt−1 is a free parameter for every period (line 106 in the parameters block). So ãt is not a parametric
  function — it is a full vector of latent states sampled by HMC.

  ---
  How the likelihood is formed

  The model has three likelihood blocks (equations (7), (8), (9)):

  Block 1 — Permanent productivity state, equation (7) (lines 309–310)

  The random walk with drift for ãt:

  mlq[t] ~ N(mu_a[i] + mlq[t-1], se1[i])

  mu_a[i] is the drift μ in regime i. This is the state equation in the state-space structure.

  Block 2 — Wage measurement equation, equation (8) (lines 312–314)

  // no capital:
  w[...] ~ N(phi[1] + lbdm[...], se2[i])

  // with capital:
  w[...] ~ N(phi[1] + lbdm[...], se2[i] ./ (1-betat[...]))

  This is the measurement equation. The noise ε2t (transitory productivity) enters wt through equation (8) as ε2t/(1-β),
  so its standard deviation is σε2/(1-β) — hence the division by (1-betat) in the with-capital case. For β=0 it reduces
  to just se2[i].

  Block 3 — Malthusian population supply, equation (9) (lines 338–341)

  n[t] ~ log_mix(p[j],
           N(omega + gamma*(w[t-1]+h[t-1]) + n[t-1] + xi1[t], sx2),  // plague year
           N(omega + gamma*(w[t-1]+h[t-1]) + n[t-1],          sx2))  // normal year

  Equation (9): nt − nt−1 = ω + γ(wt−1 + dt−1) + ξt. Plague occurs with probability p[j] and adds ξ1t ~ Beta(β1,β2) to
  population growth. This block provides the Malthusian feedback and identifies γ.

  ---
  The full likelihood is a mixture over break dates

  All three blocks are wrapped in a mixture model over the unknown first break date (lines 302–321):

  vector[dim_b] lps = log(piv);          // log prior probabilities of each break date
  for (j in 1:dim_b) {
    lps[j] += log p(wages | mu_a[i], break j) + log p(mlq | mu_a[i], break j)
  }
  target += log_sum_exp(lps);            // marginalises over break dates

  piv[j] is the posterior probability that the first break falls at date j (drawn from a Dirichlet). The log-sum-exp
  marginalises over all possible break dates.

  ---
  Summary diagram

                   state equation (eq. 7)
    dmlq[t] sampled freely  →  mlq[t] = mlq[t-1] + dmlq[t-1]  =  atilde[t]
                                            |

    lbdm[t] = f(atilde, alphat, betat, n, h, r, delta)
                                            |

  lbdm is the bridge between the latent productivity state atilde and the observed wage w. HMC jointly samples dmlq (all
